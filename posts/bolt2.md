Lightning Bolt is an interesting case example of what MTG designers call "Knobs" (insert designing magic article here).

Lightning Bolt is for one R, red instant, that says "This spell deals 3 damage to any target."

Here already we have a bunch of knobs that we can adjust! Compare instead Lightning Bolt to two <em><strong>very</strong></em> similar cards: Shock (INSERT LINK HERE) and Lightning Strike (INSERT LINK HERE).

Shock is for one R, red instant, that says "This spell deals <ins><strong>2</strong></ins> damage to any target."

Lightning Strike is for <ins><strong>1R</ins></strong>, red instant, that says "This spell deals 3 damage to any target."

We can see from these two cards we can adjust the <em>Knobs</em> of the cards Lightning Bolt; adjust the damage or the mana value respectively.

The powerlevel of these two cards compared to Lightning Bolt are drastically different however and it's not immediately evident why the subtle change of a singular value (by only +/- 1 value!) can have such a drastic change.

## What's the point? The Mana Curve.
So I make the claim that changing Lightning Bolt from 3 &rArr; 2 damage or R->1R (1->2 mana value) is something <em>drastic</em>. Why?

MTG is a game with a long history and design has grown from that history due to a number of factors, momentum being one of them. It's not a precise answer to give "It's been done like that before", but the adage (and the problem of induction) is the strong justification for why designs are made as such: Emperical Evidence suggests deviations can lead to <em>problems</em>.

This is a very vague handwavvy way of me saying that Magic design usually follows some (implicit) rules outlined by designers, as previous designers have done so, and the incentive to keep within these boundaries keeps the game in a stable position. This does not preclude that design can change or evolve: But certain elements of the game's core design do not deviate.

Tanget aside, let us consider the prototypical mana curve in Magic the Gathering.

Typically the rule of thum for creature cards in magic is that for every 1 mana you gain 1 power and 1 toughness in stats.

That is you can reasonably expect that a creature costing 1 mana should have stats around a 1/1.

For 2 mana you get 2/2s, 3 mana 3/3s, 4 mana 4/4s and so on so forth. This is not entirely a linear growth: things are usually different around 6 or 7+ mana values, but the principle that the more mana you invest in a creature the stronger it should be is a fair rule of thumb.
The more resource you invest (mana) you should proportionally get something stronger as well.

These principles have deviated over the course of Magic's history, rarely will you find a 1 mana 1/1 without any abilities (a <em>vanilla</em> creature), or without an <strong>upside</strong> (such as Keyword abilities, more on this later.)
